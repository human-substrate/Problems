# US Employment & Jobs in the AI era — can any effect be seen in the data?

**Research Study**
**Date:** 2026-09-06
**Researcher:** Daniel Miessler / Kai (LifeOS)
**Research Design:** Four-lane source discovery under a written quality filter, a fifth lane for SEC-filed platform metrics, endpoint probing, independent re-verification of every shipped value, and a deterministic level-and-trend comparison around November 30, 2022

---

## Research Question

Since ChatGPT (November 30, 2022), can any effect of AI on US jobs, hiring, and layoffs be seen in primary data? Which series — official statistics, repeated surveys, and data published by work platforms themselves — meet a quality bar high enough to publish as a dataset ([`Data/US-Employment-And-Jobs/`](../../Data/US-Employment-And-Jobs/README.md)) and as the **Employment & Jobs** section of https://usstats.io? And what do the published studies conclude?

The prompt for the study was a16z's "Charts of the Week: Experience > Skills" (September 4, 2026), which showed tech job postings asking for more years of experience and fewer skills since January 2025.

## Provenance of the a16z chart

The post credits "Source: Revelio Labs" and links no data. Revelio's free Public Labor Statistics (employment, postings, hiring and attrition, salaries; CSVs on S3) do not contain an experience-requirements or skills-count series, and Revelio's public data page offers only samples of its paid product. The chart cannot be reproduced from any public series, so it is quoted here as a claim and not turned into a row. a16z's own reading of the causal question: "while AI may have something to do with it, as well, the evidence for that is pretty weak."

## Methodology

One written filter ([METHODOLOGY.md](METHODOLOGY.md)): primary publisher only; one instrument's repeated measure; two series classes — *outcome* series need long history for a pre-AI baseline, *phenomenon* series (measures of AI itself) begin when their instrument began; native cadence stored; machine-fetchable or checked in with a citation and hash; every methodology break named; crowdsourced trackers and chart-only vendor data banned; studies are the interpretation layer and become a row only when the authors maintain a downloadable series. Every candidate's disposition, with its rule: [findings/candidates.md](findings/candidates.md). Every shipped value was re-read by the lead from the publisher.

---

## Answer

**Thirty-two series from fourteen primary publishers ship.** In the aggregates, the US labor market cooled after November 2022 without a layoff wave. In the detail most exposed to AI — programmers, software postings, call-center and data-entry work, and new graduates — the movement is large, but much of it began before ChatGPT or coincides with the 2022–2023 rate cycle and the post-pandemic technology hiring overshoot. Adoption of AI itself rose faster than any of the outcome series moved. The studies that try to attribute the movement disagree, and no official body has published a causal estimate of a US effect.

### Reading the table

**No layoff wave.** JOLTS layoffs and discharges ran 1.47M a month in November 2022 and 1.67M in July 2026, inside their 2019 range; initial jobless claims are lower than in 2019. If AI is affecting jobs so far, it is not showing up as firings in the official counts. Challenger's employer-stated AI attribution rose from 4,247 announced cuts in 2023 to 116,175 in the first eight months of 2026, but announced cuts are not realized layoffs and the reason is the employer's own.

**Hiring cooled from a peak.** Openings fell 32% and hires 18% from November 2022, temporary-help jobs fell 17%, and Indeed's total postings index fell back to its February 2020 base. Each of these had overshot in 2021–2022: openings are back near their 2019 level, while hires and temporary-help jobs sit 13–15% below it. The Fed's hiking cycle began in March 2022, before generative AI was in wide use, and the Fed and New York Fed notes quoted below read the cooling that way. Because the marker falls near that cyclical peak for hiring and postings, every "since November 2022" change on those rows is peak-to-now; the 2019 column is the offset.

**The exposed detail moved hardest, and some of it moved early.** Computer programmers fell from 199,540 (May 2019) to 92,230 (May 2025), and three-quarters of that decline (a 40% fall in the level) was already in place by May 2023 — across BLS's own caution that OEWS is not designed for year-to-year comparison, the May 2021 estimation change, and the 2018 SOC split of programmers from developers, so part of the programmer fall and developer rise may be reclassification. Software-development postings on Indeed are down 49% from November 2022 after rising 48% before it. Business-support jobs (call centers) fell 18% after November 2022 on top of 14% before. Data-entry keyers (−18%) and customer-service representatives (−9%) fell after 2023 having been roughly flat before. Software developers, the larger occupation, are still above their 2021 level. Computer-systems-design employment is down 5% from its March 2023 peak after rising every December-to-December from 2010 through 2022 (checked in the CES file).

**New graduates.** The New York Fed's recent-graduate unemployment rate rose from 4.0% to 5.7% while all-worker unemployment rose about half a point; by our read of the Fed's file, the recent-graduate minus all-worker gap in 2025–2026 is the widest since the series began in 1990. Underemployment of recent graduates only returned to its 2019 level. Stanford's Canaries index makes a related comparison inside AI-exposed occupations using ADP payroll data (rows above); it is a research index normalized to November 2022, so its change since the marker is the index minus 100 by construction, and the young cohort had risen faster than mid-career workers before the marker.

**Adoption rose on every measure.** Firms using AI to produce goods or services went from 3.7% to 10.0% in two years on Census's original question, and 17.3% to 22.4% in nine months on the reworded one; workers using AI at work went from 21% to 52% (Gallup) and 33% to 45% (Real-Time Population Survey); Indeed postings mentioning AI went from 2.3% to 6.2%; businesses paying for AI tools on Ramp from 7% to 56%.

## What the studies say

Quoted with the authors' own hedges; disagreements are left standing.

- **Stanford Digital Economy Lab, "Canaries in the Coal Mine?" (Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen; revised August 12, 2026; ADP payroll panel).** "We find no evidence of widespread, economy-wide job displacement. However, employment of young workers (ages 22–25) in AI-exposed occupations now stands 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with that of their less-exposed peers; experienced workers show no comparable gap." The mechanism is "reduced hiring of young workers rather than increased separations." Hedge: "early, descriptive indicators—canaries in the coal mine—rather than causal estimates"; the patterns "attenuate when controlling for education, show some divergent trends predating generative AI, and are more pronounced in the ADP analysis sample than in national survey benchmarks, with some evidence of consistent patterns in government administrative data." The headline changed across versions (13% regression estimate in August 2025, 16% in November 2025, 19% descriptive shortfall in August 2026).
- **Yale Budget Lab, "Tracking the Impact of AI on the Labor Market" (July 16, 2026, updated August 19, 2026; CPS).** Key takeaways as printed on the tracker page: "The occupational mix is not yet changing in ways that clearly align with the introduction of AI into the workforce. Measures of AI usage show no connection to changes in employment or unemployment. A synthetic differences-in-differences analysis of AI exposure does not yet clearly indicate an AI-related labor market footprint." The original October 2025 study's hedge: "a change over this time period simply reflects change — it does not take a stance on the cause of that change."
- **Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas (Atkinson & Yamco, January 2026; ADP and CPS).** "Workers age 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations have experienced a 13 percent decline in employment since 2022," which "would be responsible for only a 0.1 percentage point rise in aggregate unemployment since November 2022." Hedge: the pattern "may not be causal and could be driven by some other factor."
- **Federal Reserve Bank of New York (May 2026; job postings).** "The evidence from job postings provides little indication of a distinct AI-driven decline in labor demand"; it is "difficult to attribute the recent slowdown in entry-level hiring to AI alone." **Federal Reserve Board (Liu & Webber, March 2026):** "no evidence of a reduction in job postings for industries or firms which have higher levels of AI adoption."
- **Hosseini & Lichtinger, "Generative AI as Seniority-Biased Technological Change" (Harvard; Revelio résumé data, 62 million workers).** "Junior employment in adopting firms fell by about 9 percent relative to controls six quarters after the diffusion of GenAI," "driven by a slowdown in hiring, rather than by increased exits or promotions." Hedge: "These findings should be interpreted with caution … unobserved confounding factors may remain," and "if firms have overreacted to the introduction of GenAI, the resulting decline in junior employment may be temporary."
- **Hui, Reshef & Zhou (Organization Science, 2024; Upwork).** After ChatGPT, freelancers in more affected occupations saw "a decrease of 2% in the number of monthly jobs and a decrease of 5.2% in monthly earnings on the platform." Hedge: "we do not observe off-platform employment and thus cannot say whether a reduction in employment on the platform represents a reduction in total employment."
- **St. Louis Fed (Ozkan & Sullivan, August 2025).** Unemployment rose more in occupations with higher AI exposure and adoption (correlations 0.47 and 0.57). "The findings presented here represent correlation, not causation."
- **Federal Reserve Board and leadership.** Chair Powell (December 2025): AI is "probably part of the story. It's not a big part of the story yet." Vice Chair Barr (July 2026): "little evidence of economy-wide job displacement from AI." The July 2026 Monetary Policy Report lists generative AI among factors in young workers' participation decline, with no number. No Fed, BLS, CBO, CEA, IMF, or ILO publication quantifies a US AI employment effect; BLS's August 2026 AI-exposure categories carry the note "Exposure does not imply job loss."
- **Humlum & Vestergaard (Denmark, revised March 2026).** "Precise null effects on earnings and recorded hours … ruling out effects larger than 2% two years after the launch of ChatGPT." Not US data, included because it is the cleanest null.
- **Forecasts, not measurements.** Goldman Sachs (March 2026): "6–7% of workers will be displaced during that transition period," roughly "a 0.6 percentage point increase in the unemployment rate" if spread over a decade. Burning Glass Institute (July 2025) on graduates: "while cyclical factors may contribute, the evidence points to something more concerning: structural shifts." Oxford Economics (January 2026): "we haven't seen convincing signs yet that the rise is structural rather than cyclical."

The honest summary of the literature as of September 2026: the aggregate studies find no clear evidence of an economy-wide effect; the cohort and firm-level studies find early-career workers in exposed occupations and at adopting firms doing worse, from 2% (Upwork jobs) to 19% (Stanford) relative to comparison groups, with the Dallas Fed's 13% an absolute decline for the group; where a mechanism is reported it is hiring rather than firing; the descriptive studies say so outright and the quasi-experimental ones (Hosseini & Lichtinger, Hui et al., Humlum & Vestergaard) carry confounding and off-platform caveats; and the same series that show these gaps also show movement that predates generative AI.
